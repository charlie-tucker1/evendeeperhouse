# Legal/ToS posture of acquiring music for a private AI DJ-transition research project, and audio-quality verification of acquired files

Scope note: personal, non-commercial research project (deep/melodic/tech house AI transitions) run by a US (Florida) university student; current as of 7 Oct 2026. Items older than 2025 are flagged "(older)". This is a plain-language, cited map of risk and **not legal advice**; the notes separate what is *law* (statute/case law), what is *terms of service* (contract with a platform), and what is *practical risk* (how enforcement actually happens).

## KQ1. What did the 2025 US AI-training rulings (Bartz v. Anthropic, Kadrey v. Meta), the Anthropic settlement, and the Copyright Office's Part 3 report say about training on lawfully acquired vs pirated copies?

### Takeaway
Two N.D. Cal. judges (Alsup, Chhabria) held in June 2025 that copying books to train an LLM was fair use on those records, but both treated *how the copies were obtained* as decisive or at least relevant: Alsup held that building a permanent library from pirated copies was **not** fair use ("Every factor points against fair use") and that later purchases do not cure it; that piracy exposure is what Anthropic settled for $1.5B (final approval July 2026). The Copyright Office's May 2025 report says noncommercial research/analysis is the most likely-fair end of the spectrum and that unlawful access weighs against fair use. No court has yet ruled on *music* training; the UMG v. Suno/Udio cases (alleging YouTube stream-ripping) are pending.

### Cited Findings
**Bartz v. Anthropic, No. C 24-05417 WHA (N.D. Cal.), Order on Fair Use, Dkt. 231, June 23, 2025 (Alsup, J.)**
- Training holding, verbatim from the order: the use of the books "was exceedingly transformative and was a fair use under Section 107 of the Copyright Act." — [Order on Fair Use, Dkt. 231 (CourtListener PDF)](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.cand.434709/gov.uscourts.cand.434709.231.0_3.pdf)
- On the training copies: "Every factor but the nature of the copyrighted work favors this result." — [Order, Dkt. 231](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.cand.434709/gov.uscourts.cand.434709.231.0_3.pdf)
- Print-to-digital: "the digitization of the books purchased in print form by Anthropic was also a fair use" (court noted Anthropic destroyed each print copy, kept the digital copy in-house "not for sharing nor sale outside the company", and the "format change itself added no new copies, eased storage and enabled searchability"; "Anthropic purchased its print copies fair and square"). — [Order, Dkt. 231](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.cand.434709/gov.uscourts.cand.434709.231.0_3.pdf); quotes also reproduced at [Authors Alliance, June 24 2025](https://www.authorsalliance.org/2025/06/24/anthropic-wins-on-fair-use-for-training-its-llms-loses-on-building-a-central-library-of-pirated-books/)
- Pirated copies: "Anthropic had no entitlement to use pirated copies for its central library." "Creating a permanent, general-purpose library was not itself a fair use excusing Anthropic's piracy." "The downloaded pirated copies used to build a central library were not justified by a fair use." "Every factor points against fair use." Later buying a copy "will not absolve it of liability for the theft but it may affect the extent of statutory damages." The order names Books3, LibGen and PiLiMi in the factual background. — [Order, Dkt. 231](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.cand.434709/gov.uscourts.cand.434709.231.0_3.pdf)
- Additional language from the order as reproduced by Authors Alliance: "The person who copies the textbook from a pirate site has infringed already, full stop."; pirated copies cannot "be excused as fair use merely because some will eventually be used to train LLMs"; the pirated copies "plainly displaced demand for Authors' books — copy for copy"; "Bad faith is not the basis for this decision." — [Authors Alliance](https://www.authorsalliance.org/2025/06/24/anthropic-wins-on-fair-use-for-training-its-llms-loses-on-building-a-central-library-of-pirated-books/)
- The court set a trial "on the pirated copies used to create Anthropic's central library and the resulting damages." — [Order, Dkt. 231](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.cand.434709/gov.uscourts.cand.434709.231.0_3.pdf); the court also said "If the outputs seen by users had been infringing, Authors would have a different case." — [Norton Rose Fulbright](https://www.nortonrosefulbright.com/en/knowledge/publications/afb0e10b/two-us-decisions-find-that-reproducing-works-to-train-large-language-models-is-fair-use)
- Baker Botts summary of the same order: the court called training "quintessentially transformative," and said of the pirated library that "piracy was the point: To build a central library that one could have paid for... but without paying for it." — [Baker Botts, June 27 2025](https://ourtake.bakerbotts.com/post/102kpuo/court-finds-fair-use-for-ai-training-but-distinguishes-library-of-pirated-works)

**Anthropic settlement**
- Anthropic agreed in September 2025 to pay $1.5 billion, "about $3,000 per book plus interest," described as "the largest copyright resolution in U.S. history." — [Wikipedia, Bartz v. Anthropic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bartz_v._Anthropic)
- AP (July 21, 2026): District Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín approved the settlement in a Monday ruling; it covers "more than 482,000 books," about 91% already claimed; about $3,000 per book; Judge Alsup had given preliminary approval "last September" and has since retired. The AP piece frames it as a settlement over "pirated copies of books used to train" Claude, while noting Alsup found the training itself was not illegal. — [AP via ABC News, July 21 2026](https://abcnews.com/Technology/wireStory/judge-approves-15b-anthropic-settlement-pirated-books-train-134949964)

**Kadrey v. Meta Platforms, No. 3:23-cv-03417-VC (N.D. Cal.), Order, Dkt. 598, June 25, 2025 (Chhabria, J.)**
- Limits of the ruling (from the order): the ruling does not establish that Meta's training use is lawful; the plaintiffs "made the wrong arguments and failed to develop a record in support of the right one." — [Order, Dkt. 598 (CourtListener PDF)](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.cand.415175/gov.uscourts.cand.415175.598.0.pdf); same sentence quoted at [Goodwin](https://www.goodwinlaw.com/en/insights/publications/2025/06/alerts-practices-aiml-northern-district-of-california-judge-rules) and [IPWatchdog](https://ipwatchdog.com/2025/06/26/judge-says-authors-made-wrong-arguments-ruling-meta-fair-use/)
- "in many circumstances it will be illegal to copy copyright-protected works" to train generative AI without permission; companies will generally need to pay copyright holders. — [Order, Dkt. 598](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.cand.415175/gov.uscourts.cand.415175.598.0.pdf)
- Market dilution: the court distinguished direct substitution (regurgitation) from indirect substitution (outputs in the same genre that compete for sales/attention), said this harm "could weigh heavily" under factor four, but found no empirical evidence on this record; it rejected Meta's argument that only infringing outputs count. — [Order, Dkt. 598](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.cand.415175/gov.uscourts.cand.415175.598.0.pdf); Goodwin quotes the court's concern that training may harm the market "by helping to enable the rapid generation of countless works that compete with the originals." — [Goodwin](https://www.goodwinlaw.com/en/insights/publications/2025/06/alerts-practices-aiml-northern-district-of-california-judge-rules)
- Shadow-library downloads: Chhabria declined to analyze Meta's downloads separately: "Because Meta's ultimate use of the plaintiffs' books was transformative, so too was Meta's downloading of those books." Bad faith/shadow-library use "may be relevant" but did not change the result on this record. The torrenting/*distribution* claim remained: "neither side moved for summary judgment, so this will remain a live issue in the case." — [Order, Dkt. 598](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.cand.415175/gov.uscourts.cand.415175.598.0.pdf); see also [Akin Gump](https://www.akingump.com/en/insights/ai-law-and-regulation-tracker/second-district-court-rules-ai-training-can-be-fair-use)
- Chhabria criticized Alsup's analogy of training to "training schoolchildren to write well" as inapt because a model can generate countless competing works. — [Order, Dkt. 598](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.cand.415175/gov.uscourts.cand.415175.598.0.pdf)
- Status (Mar 27, 2026): second phase on "additional claims related to Meta's torrenting of books"; expert reports Sept–Nov 2026; dispositive motion hearing set for **Feb 25, 2027** before Judge Chhabria. — [chatgptiseatingtheworld.com, Mar 27 2026](https://chatgptiseatingtheworld.com/2026/03/27/kadrey-v-meta-gets-scheduling-order-summary-judgment-motion-hearing-for-distribution-claim-wont-be-heard-until-feb-25-2027/)

**US Copyright Office, Copyright and AI Part 3: Generative AI Training (pre-publication, May 9, 2025)**
- "The steps required to produce a training dataset containing copyrighted works clearly implicate the right of reproduction." (p. 26) "The training process also implicates the right of reproduction." (p. 27) — [USCO Part 3 pre-publication PDF](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-3-Generative-AI-Training-Report-Pre-Publication-Version.pdf)
- Office's fair-use spectrum (as summarized by Lowenstein): analysis is fact-specific; "Noncommercial research or analysis that doesn't enable reproduction of protected content is more likely fair"; "Commercial uses that generate competing expressive content are unlikely to qualify, especially when they involve illegal access or copies from pirate sites"; market effect is "the most significant factor when analyzing whether a use is fair"; footnote: "'[p]ublicly available' is not synonymous with 'authorized.'" — [Lowenstein Sandler, May 12 2025](https://www.lowenstein.com/news-insights/publications/client-alerts/us-copyright-office-releases-long-awaited-report-on-generative-ai-training-and-copyright-law)
- Unlawful access: the Office says training on pirated/illegally accessed works should "weigh against fair use without being determinative" (Report at 52); wholesale copying of entire works "ordinarily weighs against fair use" (at 55); "transformativeness is a matter of degree" (at 46); outputs may "displace, dilute, and erode the markets for copyrighted works" (at 65). — [Wiley, May 16 2025](https://www.wiley.law/alert-Copyright-Office-Issues-Key-Guidance-on-Fair-Use-in-Generative-AI-Training)
- Market dilution example: "If thousands of AI-generated romance novels are put on the market, fewer of the human-authored romance novels…"; licensing: recommends "allowing the voluntary licensing market for training data to continue developing," compulsory licensing only for clear market failure, extended collective licensing in limited cases. — [Lowenstein](https://www.lowenstein.com/news-insights/publications/client-alerts/us-copyright-office-releases-long-awaited-report-on-generative-ai-training-and-copyright-law); [ppc.land, May 11 2025](https://ppc.land/us-copyright-office-releases-major-ai-training-report-amid-intensifying-copyright-debate/)
- Release circumstances: issued as a *pre-publication* version on May 9, 2025; not legally binding but potentially persuasive; Register of Copyrights Shira Perlmutter was removed by the Trump administration shortly after release. — [Lowenstein](https://www.lowenstein.com/news-insights/publications/client-alerts/us-copyright-office-releases-long-awaited-report-on-generative-ai-training-and-copyright-law)

### Inferences
- For this project, the acquisition channel is the main legal variable. Bartz is the best available authority that (a) copies you *lawfully bought* and (b) use for a transformative, non-expressive purpose (learning transition statistics, not redistributing songs) sit at the favorable end; a corpus assembled from unauthorized downloads is the pattern both Alsup and the Copyright Office single out as weighing against fair use, and it is what the $1.5B settlement paid for.
- Both rulings were about books and commercial defendants; a non-commercial student project scores better on factor one, but factor four (market harm) still turns on whether outputs or the stored corpus substitute for the recordings. Keeping the corpus private and not publishing renders that contain the source recordings minimizes this.
- Chhabria's "market dilution" theory is less of a concern for a model that produces *transitions between existing tracks* than for a model that generates new tracks in the genre, but it is untested.

### Gaps
- No court has yet decided fair use for *music* training; the UMG/Sony/Warner v. Suno and v. Udio cases (which now allege stream-ripping from YouTube, per TorrentFreak below) were still pending as of the sources found. I did not find a 2026 ruling on them.
- I could not fetch the fair-use section (pp. 32–74) of the Copyright Office PDF directly; the "spectrum" language is from law-firm summaries (Lowenstein, Wiley) rather than verbatim from the report. A *final* (post-pre-publication) version was not located.
- Whether Anthropic's settlement required destruction of the pirated files was not confirmed in the sources fetched.

## KQ2. Is there any US research / text-and-data-mining (TDM) exemption, and how does that compare with the EU, UK and Japan?

### Takeaway
There is no US statutory TDM exception; US research use rides on fair use. The only US "TDM" rule is a narrow DMCA §1201 anti-circumvention *exemption* (2021, renewed/expanded Oct 2024) for academic researchers mining **literary works and films** — not sound recordings — and it only removes circumvention liability, not copyright liability. The EU (DSM Art. 3/4), UK (s.29A, non-commercial research only) and Japan (Art. 30-4, broadest) have real statutory exceptions; all require lawful access and none applies to a US-based researcher's US acts.

### Cited Findings
**United States**
- Section 1201 of the DMCA prohibits circumventing technological protection measures; the triennial rulemaking requires a showing that "the proposed use is likely to be lawful and that the digital lock makes the lawful use impractical." The Copyright Office largely granted a TDM exemption in October 2021 for researchers doing text and data mining of "literary works and films," and in 2024 renewed and expanded it to let researchers "provide access to their research corpora to researchers from outside their institutions" and view works "as needed to conduct their research." Sound recordings/music are not mentioned. — [Berkeley Law, Oct 28 2024](https://www.law.berkeley.edu/case-project/1201-exemption-text-data-mining/)
- 2024 renewal conditions as summarized by Authors Alliance: access must be credentialed and authenticated; viewing must be in furtherance of research, not for expressive value; researchers must maintain security protocols protecting corpora from unauthorized reuse and share them with rightsholders on request; trade associations may inquire on a "reasonable belief" their works are in a corpus; the collaboration requirement was removed. Federal Register document 2024-24563. — [Authors Alliance, Oct 25 2024](https://www.authorsalliance.org/2024/10/25/text-data-mining-research-dmca-exemption-renewed-and-expanded/)

**European Union (DSM Directive 2019/790)**
- Art. 3 (research exception): research organisations and cultural heritage institutions may mine for scientific research; "narrower in who can use it but stronger once it applies." Art. 4 (general exception): any beneficiary, any purpose including commercial, but rightsholders can "opt-out," which for online content must be machine-readable. "Neither exception applies where the content was unlawfully accessed"; AI providers should keep evidence of lawful access. The EU AI Act Art. 53 requires GPAI providers to have copyright-compliance policies that identify and honor Art. 4 opt-outs and to publish a training-content summary (Art. 53(1)(d)). Two German GEMA cases involve lyrics/musical works; in one, the Munich court held the general exception only covers reproductions made *before* the training process. — [Reed Smith, Sept 30 2026](https://www.reedsmith.com/articles/entertainment-media-guide-to-ai-three-years-on/text-data-mining-in-the-eu/)

**United Kingdom (CDPA s.29A)** (older: article dated July 2023)
- Requires "lawful access" (e.g., paid subscription, openly licensed material); permits computational analysis "solely for a non-commercial research purpose"; non-commercial status depends on the activity, not the organisation type; no restriction on later commercialising outputs, but an intent to commercialise at the time of analysis may defeat the non-commercial purpose. A "recent failed attempt to introduce a permissive copyright exception for TDM" is noted. — [Bristows, July 19 2023](https://www.bristows.com/news/the-text-and-data-mining-copyright-exception-in-the-uk-for-the-sole-purpose-of-research-for-a-non-commercial-purpose-what-does-it-cover/)

**Japan (Copyright Act Art. 30-4, effective Jan 1, 2019)**
- Permits use of works for "information analysis," including AI training, without consent, for commercial and non-commercial uses alike, provided the use is not for "enjoyment" of the work's thoughts/sentiments and does not "unjustly harm" the holder's interests. The Agency for Cultural Affairs' 2024 guidance (draft Jan 23, revised Feb 29, 2024) says a developer who knew or "should have known" it ingested pirated material has a factor toward contributory-infringer treatment. — [Privacy World (Squire Patton Boggs), Mar 2024](https://www.privacyworld.blog/2024/03/japans-new-draft-guidelines-on-ai-and-copyright-is-it-really-ok-to-train-ai-using-pirated-materials/)

### Inferences
- A Florida student cannot invoke EU/UK/Japan exceptions for acts done in the US; they matter mainly as comparative context and as a reason the question is unsettled.
- The US §1201 TDM exemption does not help a music project twice over: it covers literary works and films only, and it would only excuse *circumvention*, not copying.
- "Lawful access" is the common thread in every regime (EU, UK, Japan guidance, Bartz, Copyright Office): acquisition channel is the decisive variable everywhere.

### Gaps
- The exact 37 CFR 201.40(b) paragraph text and effective dates for the 2024 exemption were not fetched (Federal Register 2024-24563 is the primary source).
- UK 2024–2026 consultation outcome on an opt-out TDM model was not confirmed (Bristows article predates it).

## KQ3. Does downloading a YouTube/SoundCloud stream implicate DMCA §1201 anti-circumvention? (youtube-dl takedown 2020, Yout v. RIAA, yt-dlp today)

### Takeaway
Law: unsettled. The RIAA's position is that YouTube's "rolling cipher" signature is a technological measure and that stream-rippers circumvent it; EFF disputes that it is a lock at all. GitHub reinstated youtube-dl in Nov 2020, but in Yout v. RIAA (D. Conn. 2022) a judge held Yout had not plausibly alleged it does *not* circumvent, and the Second Circuit appeal was still pending as of Oct 2025 with Suno/Udio filing an amicus brief because the labels now accuse them of stream-ripping. Practical: yt-dlp remains actively maintained, but YouTube's bot checks ("Sign in to confirm you're not a bot", PO tokens, datacenter-IP blocks) are the real-world friction.

### Cited Findings
- Oct 2020 takedown: the RIAA argued youtube-dl violated §1201, calling it a "circumvention device" and YouTube's signature code a "rolling cipher," and argued that because it *could* be used to infringe, GitHub had to remove it. EFF responded that the signature is not a protected lock; even if it were, youtube-dl uses it as intended; YouTube's player sends a "sig" value that any JavaScript-capable client can produce; and the stream is not encrypted with a DRM scheme. GitHub reinstated the repo on Nov 16, 2020 with full functionality, acknowledged legitimate uses (accessibility, journalism, CC/public-domain video), and committed to expert review of §1201 claims and a chance to dispute before takedown. — [EFF, Nov 17 2020](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2020/11/github-reinstates-youtube-dl-after-riaas-abuse-dmca) (older)
- Yout LLC v. RIAA: in 2019 the RIAA sent anti-circumvention notices to Google claiming Yout.com circumvents YouTube's "rolling cipher" and Google delisted Yout; Yout sued for a declaration of non-infringement; in late 2022 Judge Stefan Underhill (D. Conn.) ruled Yout had not plausibly alleged it does not circumvent a technological measure under §1201(a), writing that the bypass and signature modification constitute a "process." Oral argument in the Second Circuit was in early 2024; as of Oct 2025 no decision was reported. Suno and Udio filed an amicus brief arguing Congress separated access controls (§1201(a)) from copy controls (§1201(b)) to protect fair users, that the RIAA's own notices describe the measure as preventing *copying*, and that a ruling treating the rolling cipher as an access control could give the RIAA a template against their fair-use defense — because the labels' amended complaints "now allege that training data was obtained by stream-ripping from YouTube via circumvention of the rolling cipher." — [TorrentFreak, Oct 8 2025](https://torrentfreak.com/suno-udio-wade-into-youtube-ripper-circumvention-lawsuit-appeal-251008/)
- yt-dlp practical status (2026): the `bgutil-ytdlp-pot-provider` 2.0.1 plugin (uploaded Oct 2, 2026; requires yt-dlp ≥ 2025.05.22) generates "proof-of-origin (PO) tokens" via BotGuard; it "was used to bypass the 'Sign in to confirm you're not a bot' message" on IPs YouTube has flagged, but "Providing a PO token does not guarantee bypassing 403 errors or bot checks" and "does not bypass IP-based login restrictions"; on datacenter IPs you may also need cookies. — [PyPI, bgutil-ytdlp-pot-provider](https://pypi.org/project/bgutil-ytdlp-pot-provider)

### Inferences
- Even if §1201 ultimately does not apply to YouTube's signature scheme, downloading still makes an unauthorized *reproduction* under §106 (the ordinary copyright question in KQ1), and separately breaches platform ToS (KQ4). §1201 is an *additional* layer with statutory damages of its own, and the Yout litigation shows the RIAA actively litigates it.
- The Suno/Udio amicus shows the music industry is explicitly linking stream-ripping to AI-training claims; a dataset built by yt-dlp from YouTube is the fact pattern the labels are currently attacking.
- Practical risk for a single student at low volume is mostly account/IP friction (bot checks, 403s) rather than litigation, but the legal exposure is non-trivial and the activity cannot be described as "lawfully acquired" in the Bartz sense.

### Gaps
- No Second Circuit decision in Yout v. RIAA was found as of the Oct 2025 source; whether one issued between Oct 2025 and Oct 2026 was not confirmed.
- Whether SoundCloud's stream delivery (HLS/progressive URLs) has ever been characterized as a "technological measure" in any §1201 case was not found; the SoundCloud ToS (KQ4) is the operative restriction there.

## KQ4. What do platform terms (SoundCloud, YouTube, Mixcloud, Bandcamp, Beatport, record pools) say about downloading, scraping and AI training, and how is it enforced in practice?

### Takeaway
Every platform's terms (contract, not copyright law) prohibit downloading/ripping outside the provided download features, prohibit automated access, and — in 2025–2026 wording — expressly prohibit using platform content to train AI/ML models (Mixcloud) or reserve rights against TDM (Mixcloud §11.3) and AI input (SoundCloud). Purchased downloads (Bandcamp, Beatport) are licensed "solely for personal, non-commercial use"; record-pool downloads (ZIPDJ) are "STRICTLY FOR PROMOTIONAL USE AS A PROFESSIONAL DJ" and "not for building personal music catalogs or databases." Enforcement is account termination, download revocation, and (SoundCloud) possible court action.

### Cited Findings
**SoundCloud Terms of Use — "Last Amended: August 17, 2026"**
- "You must not copy, rip or capture, or attempt to copy, rip or capture, any Content from the Platform" (other than by the offline-listening/download features where enabled); other users' content "may not be downloaded, reproduced, distributed, transmitted, re-uploaded, republished"; "You must not employ scraping or similar techniques to aggregate, repurpose, republish or otherwise make use of any Content"; restrictions on "bots, botnets, scripts, apps, plugins, extensions or other automated means"; must not "circumvent or attempt to circumvent or copy any copy protection mechanism." AI: content may not be used "for the purposes of informing, training developing (or as input to) artificial intelligence technologies" without rightsholder authorization; SoundCloud: "We will not use your content to train generative AI models that aim to replicate or synthesize your voice… without your explicit consent, which must be affirmatively provided through an opt-in mechanism." Enforcement: "SoundCloud has the right, in its sole discretion, to terminate your account or take such other action as we see fit… This may include taking court action and/or reporting offending users to the relevant authorities." — [SoundCloud Terms of Use](https://soundcloud.com/terms-of-use)
- Background to the AI clause: a Feb 2024 clause said content could be used to "inform, train, develop or serve as input to artificial intelligence"; after a May 2025 backlash, CEO Eliah Seton (letter dated May 14, 2025) replaced it with the opt-in wording above and stated "SoundCloud has never used artist content to train AI models." — [SoundCloud CEO letter, May 14 2025](https://community.soundcloud.com/playbook-articles/a-letter-from-our-ceo-clarifying-our-terms-of-use)

**YouTube Terms of Service (version dated Dec 15, 2023; captured Apr 19, 2026)**
- "You are not allowed to: access, reproduce, download, distribute, transmit, broadcast, display, sell, license, alter, modify…" any part of the Service or Content "except: (a) as expressly authorized…" — [ConductAtlas capture of YouTube ToS provision](https://conductatlas.com/platform/youtube-ads/youtube-terms-of-service/provision/CA-P-022044/prohibition-on-unauthorized-access-reproduction-distribution/); canonical page [youtube.com/t/terms](https://www.youtube.com/t/terms) (could not be fetched directly — robots.txt).

**Mixcloud Terms (no date shown; "may amend… at any time without prior notice")**
- §3: "downloading, side-loading, exchanging, creating of derivative works" are "strictly prohibited" outside the limited grant; §5.1.7: "not to obtain or attempt to obtain any Content through any means not intentionally made available"; §5.1.8: "not to use any robot, spider, scraper, or other automated means to access the Platform for any purpose"; §5.1.16: prohibits use "connected with generative artificial intelligence (AI) technologies" trained on Platform content without rightsholder authorization and specifically bans "Training any machine learning or AI model using data or content from the Platform"; §11.3: Mixcloud and rightsholders "expressly reserve" their rights against text and data mining. §2: content must have "been previously released to the public with the permission of the applicable copyright owners"; §8.2.1 puts licensing responsibility on uploaders. — [Mixcloud Terms](https://www.mixcloud.com/terms/)

**Platform stream formats (relevant to what a ripper actually gets)**

## KQ5. Output side: a rendered transition contains two copyrighted recordings — private listening vs publishing

### Takeaway
A rendered transition that still contains the source recordings is a reproduction (and arguably a derivative work) of both; keeping it private for listening tests adds little risk beyond the input copies, while publishing it is the highest-risk step. Platforms handle DJ mixes by *licensing* (Mixcloud's stream-only model; SoundCloud's 2016 GEMA deal) rather than by exception, and the licences are the platform's, not the uploader's.

### Cited Findings
- Mixcloud's licence "covers only streaming" and it "prohibits downloads for licensing reasons"; DJ shows are permitted because the platform (not the uploader) holds collecting-society/label licences; uploaders must tag tracklists correctly. — [Wikipedia, Mixcloud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixcloud) (sources 2009–2018, older)
- Mixcloud §2 requires that audio in shows has "been previously released to the public with the permission of the applicable copyright owners," and §8.2.1 makes the uploader warrant "you have all necessary licenses, rights, consents, and permissions." — [Mixcloud Terms](https://www.mixcloud.com/terms/)
- SoundCloud (Dec 2016, older): founder Eric Wahlforss said an agreement with GEMA meant "DJ mixes can now be legally and problem-free on SoundCloud" for subscribers and non-subscribers alike; the only territory named is Germany. — [Mixmag, Dec 12 2016](https://mixmag.net/amp/soundcloud-mixes-will-not-be-hit-with-copyright-claims-anymore)
- Bartz: "If the outputs seen by users had been infringing, Authors would have a different case." — [Norton Rose Fulbright](https://www.nortonrosefulbright.com/en/knowledge/publications/afb0e10b/two-us-decisions-find-that-reproducing-works-to-train-large-language-models-is-fair-use)
- Copyright Office: "Noncommercial research or analysis that doesn't enable reproduction of protected content is more likely fair." — [Lowenstein](https://www.lowenstein.com/news-insights/publications/client-alerts/us-copyright-office-releases-long-awaited-report-on-generative-ai-training-and-copyright-law)

### Inferences
- Private renders used only for your own listening/evaluation do not "enable portions of the works to be reproduced" to anyone else and keep you on the favorable side of both Alsup's and the Copyright Office's framing; publishing renders that contain the recordings forfeits that.
- If a demo must be public, the lowest-risk routes are (i) Mixcloud (platform-licensed, stream-only, with full tracklist metadata), or (ii) rendering transitions with royalty-free / self-produced / CC-licensed tracks. YouTube/SoundCloud uploads of full-recording transitions will be subject to Content ID / fingerprint claims, which may block, mute, or monetize for the rightsholder (specific 2025–2026 Content ID behaviour for DJ mixes was not found in the fetched sources — see Gaps).

## KQ7. Quality (a): how to detect transcoded "fake 320"/fake lossless files and measure effective bandwidth — methods, tools, cutoff tables

### Takeaway
The standard method is spectral-cutoff analysis (where the spectrogram goes dark relative to the midband), cross-checked against the container's declared bitrate and the encoder's known lowpass defaults. LAME's defaults are the key reference (128 kbps → 17.0 kHz, 192 → 18.6 kHz, 320 → 20.5 kHz); Opus is *always* 20 kHz "fullband" regardless of bitrate, so a 20 kHz shelf does not mean "320 MP3." Cutoff is "neither necessary nor sufficient": genuine old recordings can roll off at 16 kHz and modern AAC leaves fewer spectral "holes." Spek (last release 2023) and Fakin' The Funk? (Windows) are the usual GUI tools; Lossless Audio Checker is gone; auCDtect fails at high bitrates.

### Cited Findings
**Encoder lowpass defaults (the lookup table)**
- LAME source (`optimum_bandwidth`, used for CBR and ABR): `{64, 11000}, {80, 13500}, {96, 15100}, {112, 15600}, {128, 17000}, {160, 17500}, {192, 18600}, {224, 19400}, {256, 19700}, {320, 20500}` (bitrate kbps → lowpass Hz; lower entries: 8→2000, 16→3700, 24→3900, 32→5500, 40→7000, 48→7500, 56→10000). VBR (`-V0`…`-V9`) uses a separate hard-coded array in `lame_init_params()`; for the default VBR mode it is `{19500, 19000, 18500, 18000, 17500, 16500, 15500, 14500, 12500, 9500, 3950}` (V0→19.5 kHz, V2→18.5 kHz, V5→16.5 kHz …), and for `vbr_rh` `{19500, 19000, 18600, 18000, 17500, 16000, 15600, 14900, 12500, 10000, 3950}`. — [LAME lame.c (rbrito mirror)](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/rbrito/lame/master/libmp3lame/lame.c)
- LAME docs: "Using a lowpass filter helps reducing the amount of data to encode"; "The default value depends on the target bitrate/quality"; `--lowpass` range "[0.001..50]kHz or [50..50000]Hz"; `--lowpass-width` default is 75% of a band width (1/64 of the sample rate). — [LAME detailed settings](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/lame@1.2.4/deps/lame/doc/html/detailed.html)
- Opus bandwidth constants: NARROWBAND "4 kHz bandpass", MEDIUMBAND "6 kHz", WIDEBAND "8 kHz", SUPERWIDEBAND "12 kHz", FULLBAND "20 kHz bandpass." — [Opus API 1.5 docs](https://opus-codec.org/docs/opus_api-1.5/group__opus__ctlvalues.html)
- iTunes/QuickTime AAC (older, QT 6.4 era): "At 192 kbps, QT lowpass is set up to 17750 Hz"; 224 kbps ≈ 18 kHz; 256 kbps ≈ 20 kHz (44.1 kHz stereo); "Lame APS goes higher in frequency." — [Hydrogenaudio thread "low pass frequencies in iTunes AAC"](https://hydrogenaudio.org/index.php/topic,15839.0.html)
- Rule-of-thumb cutoffs circulated for MP3: 11 kHz = 64 kbps; 16 kHz = 128 kbps; 19 kHz = 192 kbps; 20 kHz = 320 kbps — with the caveat that the author's genuine 1980s ADD-era CD rips also stop at 16 kHz, "so a low cutoff doesn't prove a file is fake." — [The Well-Tempered Computer, "Lossless, hi-res or fake" (page dated Sept 20 2026)](https://thewelltemperedcomputer.com/SW/AudioTools/Detect.htm)
- Vorbis: a 2024 study notes "Vorbis's default 16 kHz cutoff made it easiest to detect" in a naive cutoff-driven classifier. — [arXiv 2407.21545](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.21545v1)

**Why cutoff alone is unreliable (research evidence)**
- "Robust lossy audio compression identification" (arXiv 2407.21545, July 2024): CNN+BiLSTM on 2-s mono 44.1 kHz spectrogram windows; trained on 10k tracks encoded with ffmpeg in MP3 (libmp3lame), AAC (libfdk_aac) and Vorbis at 128/256/320 kbps. A naive model hit ~99.8% on default-cutoff files but, when cutoffs were varied among 14/16/18/20 kHz, lossy-detection accuracy fell ~70 points for AAC, ~30 for MP3, ~10 for Vorbis; saliency showed the naive model "focused mainly on the cutoff frequency rather than the spectral 'holes'." A model trained with random spectral masks (14 kHz–Nyquist) recovered 96.8% on lossy / 99.8% on lossless (98.4% overall), AAC remaining weakest (81% at 14 kHz cutoff). Authors conclude cutoff is "neither necessary nor sufficient"; "Quiet, sparse lossless tracks with little high-frequency energy were misclassified as lossy." Opus was not tested. — [arXiv 2407.21545](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.21545v1)
- Well-Tempered Computer's overall conclusion: there are no reliable techniques for telling high-bitrate MP3 from lossless; detection programs are "completely at loss" at high bitrates. — [Well-Tempered Computer](https://thewelltemperedcomputer.com/SW/AudioTools/Detect.htm)

**Tools**
- **Spek**: "acoustic spectrum analyser written in C++", FFmpeg decoding + wxWidgets GUI; BSD/Linux/Windows/macOS; GPL-3.0; latest release 0.8.5 (2023-01-09); README binaries still link v0.8.2; no newer dated activity visible. — [github.com/alexkay/spek](https://github.com/alexkay/spek)
- **Fakin' The Funk?** (as of 2017 review; older): Windows-only; batch-scans a library, flags files whose "stated bitrate or peak frequency doesn't match their actual quality" (e.g., "a 128kbps MP3 re-encoded as 320kbps"), shows the "actual bitrate," built-in spectrum analyzer showing each file's frequency cut-off; formats MP3, MP4/M4A, OGG, OPUS, FLAC, WMA, AAC, ALAC, MPC, SPX, TTA, WAV; free version limited to 100 fakes, full version via "€9.99 ($10.75 USD)" donation; claims it can't be fooled by audio optimizers like Platinum Notes. — [PCDJ, Mar 29 2017](https://pcdj.com/batch-detect-the-true-quality-of-your-audio-files/); in a 2026 test it correctly identified a genuine 1985/86 lossless recording as lossless. — [Well-Tempered Computer](https://thewelltemperedcomputer.com/SW/AudioTools/Detect.htm)
- **Lossless Audio Checker**: detected "upscaling, upsampling and transcoding" (AAC only in early version), "very much WIP"; "As of 2024 it was no longer available," site redirects to a Qobuz DSD promotion. — [Well-Tempered Computer](https://thewelltemperedcomputer.com/SW/AudioTools/Detect.htm)
- **auCDtect**: free console tool claiming to tell original CD audio from audio "reconstructed" from a lossy source; fails at high bitrates. — [Well-Tempered Computer](https://thewelltemperedcomputer.com/SW/AudioTools/Detect.htm)
- **VerifAI Audio**: AI-based fake-lossless detector; in the author's test it misreported a genuine lossless recording as lossy (<128 kbps) and reported 48 kHz for a 44.1 kHz file. — [Well-Tempered Computer](https://thewelltemperedcomputer.com/SW/AudioTools/Detect.htm)

### Inferences
- A Python recipe consistent with the above (my synthesis, not a cited finding): decode to mono float at native rate (`ffmpeg`/`soundfile`), STFT (n_fft 4096, Hann), take the long-term average power spectrum over the loudest 25% of frames (so quiet intros don't bias it), reference level = median power in 1–5 kHz, define **effective bandwidth** as the highest frequency bin whose level is within X dB (try X = 40–50 dB) of that reference *and* whose level stays within X dB for a contiguous run above it; compare to the table: ~16–17 kHz → likely 128 kbps-class; ~18.5–19.5 → 192–256 class; ~20–20.5 → 320/V0 or Opus/high-rate AAC; ≥ 21 kHz with energy to Nyquist → plausibly lossless. Then (i) read the container bitrate (`ffprobe -show_streams`), (ii) check the LAME tag lowpass field (mp3guessenc-style tools read it), and (iii) flag mismatches (declared 320 but shelf at 16–17 kHz = transcode; FLAC with 19–20 kHz shelf = fake lossless; a 20 kHz shelf in a `.opus`/`.webm` is *normal*).
- Because quiet or old material can legitimately roll off early (per both the 2024 paper and the Well-Tempered page), treat the cutoff as a *screen* and confirm with a second cue: look for the MP3 "holes"/speckled sparse high band in a spectrogram vs a smooth shelf; or look for the characteristic LAME lowpass-width taper (75% of a 1/64-sample-rate band, i.e., ~517 Hz wide at 44.1 kHz).
- HE-AAC (SBR) files show a synthetic, copied-looking band above the crossover (typically ~8–11 kHz at 32–64 kbps); the Rao & Peters paper (KQ8) treats HE-AAC v1 at 16–32 kbps as a distinct, severe degradation class, consistent with this.

### Gaps
- Could not fetch mp3guessenc's homepage (permission prompt) or the official Fakin' The Funk? site (permission prompt); current version/price of Fakin' The Funk? are from a 2017 review.
- No source was found giving measured AAC-LC lowpass defaults for current encoders (libfdk_aac, Apple AAC 2020s) at 128/256 kbps; the only AAC numbers found are 2000s-era QuickTime values. The user's "AAC-LC 128/256 → ~16/19 kHz" figures remain unverified.
- No authoritative source on Mixcloud's codec (HE-AAC 64 kbps) was found.

## KQ8. Quality (b): measured effects of lossy codecs on MIR/DSP (beat tracking, features, separation, transients, key)

### Takeaway
Published evidence says standard MIR descriptors are robust at ≥160 kbps MP3 (MFCC error converges by 160–256 kbps; chroma even more robust after normalization), beat tracking kept median F-measure > 0.85 even at 64 kbps MP3, but very low bitrates (32 kbps MP3, 16–32 kbps HE-AAC) can cut a CNN classifier's accuracy by up to 57% unless the model saw coded audio in training. Pre-echo on sharp transients is a known MDCT-codec artifact mitigated by short blocks. No study specifically measuring Demucs/HT-Demucs SDR on MP3/AAC/Opus *inputs* was found.

### Cited Findings
- **Urbano, Bogdanov, Herrera, Gómez, Serra (ISMIR 2014)** — 400 tracks (30-s excerpts, 395 artists, 10 genres); WAV vs MP3 CBR/VBR at 44.1 and 22.05 kHz; Essentia and QM Vamp. MFCC relative error ~6.1% (22.05 kHz) / 6.7% (44.1 kHz) on average, converging to ~3% at 256 kbps (Essentia) and ~5% at 160–192 kbps (QM); chroma ~6% relative error (Essentia), QM more robust, and normalizing chroma vectors removed bitrate-dependent magnitude effects; genre-classification accuracy differences across encodings ~0.02 and mostly not significant; conclusion: MFCCs are robust "provided that a minimum bitrate of approximately 160 Kbps is used," chroma even more so; "a minimum audio quality might be needed for some descriptors." — [ISMIR 2014 paper PDF](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2014/paper/000326.pdf)
- **Mauch & Ewert, Audio Degradation Toolbox (ISMIR 2013)** — degradations include "Strong MP3 Compression" (CBR 64 kbps), Live Recording, Radio Broadcast, Smartphone Playback/Recording, Vinyl; tasks: audio ID, score-to-audio alignment, beat tracking (BeatRoot, Davies), chord detection (Chordino, HPA). For most degradations "including MP3," median beat-tracking F-measures stayed above 0.85; the worst case was Live Recording (BeatRoot 0.92→0.65; Davies 0.94→0.77), attributed to spurious onsets from the room impulse response; the onset-free Davies tracker was less affected. GPL toolbox at code.soundsoftware.ac.uk. — [ISMIR 2013 paper PDF](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2013/paper/000145.pdf)
- **Rao & Peters, "On the Effect of Coding Artifacts on Acoustic Scene Classification" (arXiv 2112.04841, Erlangen/AudioLabs, DCASE-related)** — a model trained on uncompressed audio (0.820 accuracy) dropped to 0.352 on 32 kbps MP3 (−57.1% relative) and to 0.741 on 64 kbps AAC-LC (−9.6%); conditions included HE-AAC v1 32 kbps, Opus 32 kbps, SBC 64 kbps. Retraining with coded audio (MP3 64k, HE-AAC 16k, AAC 32k, etc.) raised MP3-32k (unseen bitrate) from 0.301 to 0.598 (+98.7%), AAC-32k 0.558→0.670, MP3-64k 0.615→0.685, Opus-64k (unseen codec) 0.638→0.650 (+1.9%), SBC-64k 0.555→0.589; average +24.1% across seven codec conditions, every coded condition reaching ≥81% of clean accuracy; clean accuracy unchanged (0.721→0.720). Gains on *unseen* codecs were modest. — [arXiv 2112.04841](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.04841)
- **Pre-echo**: an artifact where noise is heard *before* a transient; "most noticeable in sharp, impulsive sounds (transients) from percussion instruments"; arises in MDCT codecs (MP3, AAC, Vorbis) from "quantization noise being spread over the entire transform window of the codec"; audible because backward temporal masking is much weaker than forward masking; mitigated by "short blocks in MP3" (temporary smaller transform windows) and bit-reservoir/impulse tuning in oggenc. — [Wikipedia, Pre-echo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-echo)
- **Source separation and codecs**: the Music Source Restoration paper (Zang, Dai, Plumbley, Kong, arXiv 2505.21827, May 2025) models "lossy codec" as one of five production degradations (with spectral filtering, dynamic range compression, harmonic distortion, reverb) in its training pipeline on the RawStems dataset (578 songs, 354 h), but does not evaluate Demucs/HT-Demucs/BS-RoFormer on compressed inputs. — [arXiv 2505.21827](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.21827)
- MUSDB18-HQ "is the uncompressed version of the MUSDB18 dataset… saved as uncompressed wav files… stereophonic and encoded at 44.1kHz," created "as a reference database for the design and the evaluation of source separation algorithms." — [Zenodo 3338373](https://zenodo.org/records/3338373)

### Inferences
- For *beat-grid timing*: the ADT result (64 kbps MP3 still > 0.85 median F) plus the dominance of the 20–200 Hz kick band (which no codec lowpasses) implies beat/downbeat tracking on 128–320 kbps house music is unlikely to be the bottleneck; pre-echo can slightly smear onset *energy* before the kick at low bitrates, which could bias onset-based trackers a few ms early, but no measured number for this was found.
- For *stems separation*: separators trained on uncompressed 44.1 kHz stems (MUSDB18-HQ-style) will see a mismatched, band-limited, "holey" high band on 128 kbps inputs; by analogy to Rao & Peters, expect the hi-hat/air content of drum and "other" stems to degrade most, bass least. Treat separation quality on ≤160 kbps inputs as unvalidated and benchmark it yourself.
- For *key detection*: Urbano's finding that chroma is the most robust feature (after normalization) suggests key estimation is largely insensitive to bitrate above ~128 kbps; no direct key-detection-vs-bitrate study was found.
- Opus and modern AAC at ≥128 kbps retain full 20 kHz bandwidth, so "effective bandwidth" is not the right quality proxy for them; their degradation shows up as fine-structure quantization noise and transient smearing rather than a missing band.

### Gaps
- No study found that measures Demucs/HT-Demucs/BS-RoFormer SDR on MP3/AAC/Opus-compressed mixtures (2022–2026). This is a real gap; the project should run its own A/B (lossless vs 320/192/128 re-encodes of the same tracks).
- No quantitative study found on pre-echo's effect on onset/beat timing for electronic kicks specifically.
- No 2022–2026 paper found that repeats the ADT beat-tracking evaluation with modern trackers (madmom, BeatThis) under MP3/AAC/Opus at 96–320 kbps.
- The MUSDB18 (non-HQ) AAC bitrate and "~16 kHz" bandwidth claim were not confirmed by the Zenodo page fetched.

## KQ9. Quality (c): recommended ingest QA policy and the "codec-matching" idea; prior art on codec augmentation

### Takeaway
Policy that follows from the evidence: (1) library for mixing/listening tests — lossless or genuine 320/V0, verified by cutoff + metadata; (2) feature mining (beat grid, key, energy) — ≥160–192 kbps genuine files are fine; (3) pretraining a critic/encoder — mixed bitrates are acceptable *only* if you control for codec as a nuisance variable; and (4) **codec-match your own renders** (re-encode at the corpus's codec/bitrate before feature extraction or critic scoring) and/or codec-augment training, which Rao & Peters show restores most accuracy on seen codecs but transfers only modestly to unseen ones.

### Cited Findings
- Minimum bitrate for descriptor robustness: "a minimum bitrate of approximately 160 Kbps" for MFCCs; chroma more robust. — [Urbano et al., ISMIR 2014](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2014/paper/000326.pdf)
- Beat tracking survives 64 kbps MP3 with median F > 0.85. — [Mauch & Ewert, ISMIR 2013](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2013/paper/000145.pdf)
- Codec augmentation prior art: retraining with coded audio raised average accuracy 24.1% across seven codec conditions with no loss on clean audio; largest gains on seen codecs/bitrates (MP3 32k +98.7%), small on unseen codecs (Opus +1.9%, SBC +6.1%); authors note augmentation increases training time. — [Rao & Peters, arXiv 2112.04841](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.04841)
- Codec as a simulated degradation in a music-separation training pipeline (lossy codec among five degradation types). — [Zang et al., arXiv 2505.21827](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.21827)
- A classifier can latch onto the cutoff frequency as a shortcut; random spectral masking during training prevented that shortcut and improved generalization across cutoffs. — [arXiv 2407.21545](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.21545v1)
- Stream-ripped SoundCloud audio is at most 160 kbps AAC for free listeners (96 kbps for sub-160k sources). — [SoundCloud Help](https://help.soundcloud.com/hc/en-us/articles/53385714099611-Audio-Streaming-Formats)

### Inferences
- **Per-use minimums (synthesis):** listening tests / human preference studies → lossless or verified 320/V0 only (so raters judge the transition, not the codec); DJ mixing library → same; beat-grid/key/energy mining → genuine ≥160 kbps MP3 or ≥128 kbps AAC/Opus acceptable; stems separation → prefer lossless/320 (unvalidated below); pretraining a transition critic → any genuine ≥128 kbps *if* codec/bitrate is logged as metadata and balanced across classes.
- **Why codec-matching matters:** if the corpus of "good" transitions is 128–160 kbps rips and your renders are lossless, a critic can trivially separate "real" from "rendered" by bandwidth or MP3 holes (exactly the shortcut the 2024 identification paper's saliency maps revealed). Re-encoding every render through the same codec/bitrate distribution as the corpus before scoring removes that cue; codec augmentation of the corpus (randomly re-encoding real examples at several bitrates) is the training-side complement, with the caveat from Rao & Peters that unseen codecs still hurt — so augment with the actual codecs present (MP3, AAC-LC, Opus) rather than one.
- **Ingest pipeline (synthesis):** `ffprobe` declared codec/bitrate/sample rate → compute effective bandwidth (KQ7 recipe) → compare to LAME/Opus tables → tag each file with {declared_bitrate, est_bandwidth, codec, "transcode_suspect"} → exclude transcode-suspect files from listening tests and from the "lossless" tier; keep them, labeled, for mining. Record provenance (purchase receipt / feed URL) alongside, since provenance is also the legal variable (KQ1, KQ6).
- Buying from Beatport/Bandcamp solves both problems at once: it is the lowest legal-risk tier *and* the only channel that reliably delivers lossless or genuine 320 kbps.

### Gaps
- No paper found that specifically applies codec augmentation to *music* MIR tasks (tagging, beat tracking, separation) with 2022–2026 models; the strongest evidence is from acoustic scene classification (Rao & Peters) and from generic separation-degradation modeling (MSR paper).
- No published "ingest QA policy" for MIR datasets was found; the per-use minimums above are inference from the cited robustness results.
